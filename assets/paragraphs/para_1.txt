Accounting is often described as the language of business, and for good reason. It is the systematic process of recording, classifying, summarizing, and interpreting financial transactions to provide meaningful information to stakeholders. Every organization, whether a small family-run shop or a multinational corporation, depends on accounting to keep track of its financial health. Without accurate accounting, businesses would operate blindly, unable to know whether they are making profits or suffering losses, how much they owe, or how much they are owed.
The foundations of modern accounting trace back centuries, with the double-entry bookkeeping system developed in fifteenth-century Italy by Luca Pacioli still forming the backbone of the discipline today. Under this system, every transaction is recorded in at least two accounts — as a debit in one and a credit in another — ensuring that the books always balance. This elegant principle of checks and balances is what makes accounting such a reliable tool for financial record-keeping. From this foundation, the entire framework of financial statements has evolved, including the income statement, which reveals profitability over a period; the balance sheet, which presents a snapshot of assets, liabilities, and equity at a given moment; and the cash flow statement, which tracks the movement of cash into and out of the business.
